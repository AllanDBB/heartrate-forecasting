\begin{abstract}
Accurate multi-step heart rate (HR) forecasting during physical activity is
essential for sports performance monitoring and health risk assessment.  We
propose an ensemble framework built on six locally trained deep learning
architectures: TCN, N-BEATS, LSTM, TiDE, Encoder-Decoder LSTM, and
iTransformer.  Four ensemble strategies are evaluated under a strict
anti-leakage protocol: simple averaging, optimisation-based weighted
combination, Ridge regression stacking, and a novel Artificial Immune System
adaptive ensemble (AIS-Ensemble).  Experiments are conducted on a real-world
multi-subject exercise dataset of 39 subjects across 10 structured sessions at
two forecast horizons ($L \in \{45, 200\}$ steps).  Results show that Ridge
stacking achieves an 11.9\% relative MAPE reduction over the simple-average
baseline at the 45-step horizon (21.6\% at 200 steps), and dominates across
all 31 non-empty model subsets in an exhaustive permutation search.  The
AIS-Ensemble introduces per-subject adaptation and out-of-distribution detection
via negative selection, providing subject-level predictability insights beyond
what static ensemble methods offer.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
heart rate forecasting, time series, deep learning, foundation models, ensemble
learning, artificial immune system, stacking
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Heart rate (HR) is one of the most informative physiological signals for
exercise intensity quantification, fatigue monitoring, and cardiovascular health
assessment \cite{achten2003}. The ability to forecast future HR trajectories in
real time---given a recent measurement window---would enable proactive training
load control, early detection of overexertion, and personalised pacing
strategies.

Classical approaches based on ARIMA or linear regression \cite{box2015} fail
to capture the nonlinear, non-stationary dynamics of exercise-induced HR
responses. Deep learning---convolutional, recurrent, and attention-based
architectures---has substantially improved accuracy on physiological time
series \cite{nakamura2020}. More recently, time series foundation models
such as MOMENT \cite{goswami2024moment} and Moirai \cite{woo2024moirai}, pretrained
on large corpora, raise the question of whether domain-agnostic representations
can compete with specialised local models. Independently, ensemble methods
have proven consistently superior to individual models in forecasting
competitions \cite{makridakis2018m4}, yet naive averaging can be harmful when
model quality is highly non-uniform.

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
  \item Systematic comparison of six deep learning architectures for
        multi-step HR forecasting at two horizon lengths ($L \in \{45, 200\}$).
  \item Evaluation of four ensemble strategies under a rigorous anti-leakage
        protocol including an exhaustive search over all 31 non-empty subsets of
        five base models.
  \item A novel AIS-based adaptive ensemble that adjusts combination weights
        per subject using immunological memory and detects out-of-distribution
        windows via negative selection.
  \item Empirical evidence that Ridge stacking consistently dominates
        optimisation-based weighting across all model subsets and horizon lengths.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Heart Rate Prediction}
Early physiological forecasting relied on ARIMA and linear regression
\cite{box2015}. LSTM and CNN approaches substantially improved accuracy on
exercise HR by capturing nonlinear temporal patterns \cite{nakamura2020}.
Multi-step forecasting remains harder than single-step due to error
accumulation across the horizon.

\subsection{Foundation Models for Time Series}
MOMENT \cite{goswami2024moment} and Moirai \cite{woo2024moirai} are
transformer-based models pretrained on large time series corpora. Their benefit
in physiological domains is unclear: pretraining may not cover exercise-induced
dynamics, but learned representations can provide a useful inductive bias,
especially for data-scarce subjects.

\subsection{Ensemble Methods}
Ensemble diversity drives improvement over individual models \cite{makridakis2018m4}.
Stacking \cite{wolpert1992} trains a meta-learner on held-out predictions,
enabling horizon-dependent combination. Mixture-of-experts \cite{jacobs1991}
and algorithm selection \cite{rice1976} weight models by input features,
motivating our AIS-Ensemble.

\subsection{Artificial Immune Systems}
AIS are bio-inspired frameworks \cite{dasgupta1999}. The aiNet algorithm
\cite{decastro2000} builds a compact memory-cell network via clonal selection
and hypermutation. Applications span clustering and anomaly detection, but
their use as adaptive ensemble mechanisms for physiological forecasting is novel.

\section{Dataset and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:data}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Data Collection}
The dataset comprises HR time series from \textbf{39 subjects} across
\textbf{10 structured exercise sessions} (FTP ramp tests, base conditioning,
and HIIT protocols). Each series contains 1,841 timesteps at a uniform
sampling frequency (HR in bpm), yielding 132 series in total.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
Each series is independently standardised (zero mean, unit variance) using
parameters estimated only from the training partition. Parameters are stored
and used to restore predictions to bpm before metric computation.

\subsection{Data Partitioning and Windowing}
The data is split chronologically: \textbf{70\% training} (91 series) for
base model training, and \textbf{30\% evaluation} (39 series). The evaluation
partition is further split into two equal halves:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textit{ens\_fit}: $\approx$18,256--24,301 windows (varies with $L$),
        used to fit ensemble weights, the Ridge meta-learner, and AIS memory.
  \item \textit{held\_out}: $\approx$18,257--24,302 windows, used exclusively
        for final metric computation.
\end{itemize}

Sliding-window instances are generated with $\Lin = \Lout \in \{45, 200\}$
and stride 1. All individual predictions are cached before any ensemble
fitting, so no ensemble method accesses held-out targets during training.

\section{Models}
\label{sec:models}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Local Deep Learning Models}
All six local models follow a sequence-to-sequence paradigm: given $\Lin$
standardised HR values, predict the next $\Lout$ values. They are trained
exclusively on the 70\% partition with inner 70/10/20 train/val/test splits.

\textbf{TCN} \cite{bai2018} uses dilated causal convolutions (dilations
$\{1,2,4,8\}$, kernel size 12, 32 filters) with residual connections.

\textbf{N-BEATS} \cite{oreshkin2020} decomposes time series into trend and
seasonality via stacked fully connected blocks with learned basis expansions.

\textbf{LSTM} uses two stacked layers with gating mechanisms for long-range
dependencies.

\textbf{TiDE} \cite{das2024tide} encodes input history into a compact dense
vector and decodes it via MLP layers.

\textbf{EncDec} encodes the context with one LSTM and decodes it
autoregressively with a second LSTM.

\textbf{iTransformer} \cite{liu2024itransformer} applies self-attention over
the variate dimension rather than time. In the univariate setting this reduces
to standard attention over features.

\subsection{Foundation Models}
\textbf{MOMENT} (\texttt{MOMENT-1-large}, $\approx$300M parameters)
\cite{goswami2024moment} is fine-tuned on the 70\% partition (context 512,
5 epochs, $\mathrm{lr}=5{\times}10^{-4}$, AdamW, forecasting head only).

\textbf{Moirai} (\texttt{moirai-1.1-R-small}) \cite{woo2024moirai} is
fine-tuned with patch size 32; the median of 20 Monte Carlo samples gives the
point forecast.

\section{Ensemble Methods}
\label{sec:ensemble}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Simple Average}
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}_{\mathrm{avg}}(t) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{y}_i(t).
\end{equation}
Equal weights serve as a variance-reduction baseline requiring no fitting.

\subsection{Optimisation-Based Weighted Average}
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}_{\mathrm{opt}}(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i\hat{y}_i(t),\quad
  w_i\ge0,\;\textstyle\sum_i w_i=1,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{w}$ minimises MAPE on \textit{ens\_fit} via Nelder-Mead
\cite{nelder1965} with weights projected onto the probability simplex. The
optimiser can zero out underperforming models.

\subsection{Ridge Regression Stacking}
A Ridge meta-learner ($\alpha=1.0$) is trained on the
$N{\times}\Lout$-dimensional concatenated predictions over \textit{ens\_fit}:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}_{\mathrm{stack}}(t)=
  \mathbf{W}^{\!\top}\![\hat{y}_1;\ldots;\hat{y}_N]+\mathbf{b}.
\end{equation}
Unlike static weights, stacking learns horizon-dependent combination.

\subsection{AIS-Adaptive Ensemble}
The AIS-Ensemble adapts combination weights per context window and per subject
via five components.

\textbf{Feature extraction.} A 20-dimensional vector $\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)$
is extracted per window: basic statistics (mean, std, min, max, range), shape
(skewness, kurtosis, CV), trend (slope, curvature), autocorrelation at lags
$\{1,5,10,20\}$, spectral features (dominant frequency, spectral entropy),
activity (zero-crossing rate, peak density), and percentiles (Q25, Q75).

\textbf{aiNet clustering.} $K=30$ memory antibodies $\{c_k\}$ are learned from
\textit{ens\_fit} features via clonal selection (5 clones per antibody),
somatic hypermutation, and network suppression ($\sigma_s=0.3$) over 50
iterations.

\textbf{Affinity.} For a test window with features $\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)$:
\begin{equation}
  a_k = \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{\|\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)-c_k\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
  \quad\sigma=1.0.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Subject memory.} For each subject $i$, a matrix $M_i\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times N}$
is built on \textit{ens\_fit} recording which model predicted best per cluster.
$M_i[k,m]$ is normalised to a probability distribution.

\textbf{Inference.}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{w}^*(i,x)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\Bigl(\textstyle\sum_k a_k M_i[k,:]\Bigr),
  \quad
  \hat{y}_{\mathrm{AIS}}=\textstyle\sum_m w^*_m\hat{y}_m.
\end{equation}
A KD-tree on \textit{ens\_fit} flags windows beyond the 99th-percentile
distance as out-of-distribution (nonself); these fall back to TCN.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
All metrics are computed on \textit{held\_out} in the original bpm scale:
MAPE (mean absolute percentage error, \%), MAPE\textsubscript{med} (median
MAPE, robust to outliers), DTW (Dynamic Time Warping, Sakoe-Chiba $w=20$),
Pearson (mean correlation), and Pearson\textsubscript{med} (median
correlation).

\subsection{Permutation Search}
An exhaustive search evaluates all 31 non-empty subsets of five models (TCN,
N-BEATS, LSTM, TiDE, EncDec; iTransformer excluded due to poor individual
performance) under both optimisation and stacking, at $L=200$.

\subsection{AIS-Ensemble Analysis}
\label{ssec:ais}

The AIS-Ensemble was evaluated on the $L=200$ configuration. Two qualitative
findings emerge from the trained model diagnostics.

\textbf{Out-of-distribution detection.} Approximately 10--20\% of test windows
per subject are flagged as nonself by the KD-tree negative selector, indicating
that held-out exercise patterns are not always covered by the ens\_fit
calibration distribution. These windows fall back to TCN.

\textbf{Per-subject weight diversity.} Subject memory matrices $M_i$ differ
substantially across subjects: subjects with steady-state HR profiles show
$M_i$ concentrated on TCN, while subjects with more complex recovery patterns
distribute weight across TCN and EncDec, confirming that the per-subject
calibration captures genuine inter-individual differences in model suitability.

The nonself ratio per subject correlates with inter-subject variability in
individual model accuracy, suggesting that OOD detection can serve as a
deployment-time reliability indicator beyond aggregate MAPE.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\textbf{Why stacking beats optimisation.} The Ridge meta-learner learns
horizon-dependent weights: it can favour TCN at short forecast steps and EncDec
at later steps. The optimised simplex weights must use a single scalar per
model across the entire horizon, an inherent expressivity limitation. At
$L=200$, this matters more because different models capture dynamics at
different temporal scales.

\textbf{TiDE's role in ensembles.} TiDE is a mediocre individual model (MAPE
5.75\% and 5.80\% at $L=45$ and $L=200$), yet it consistently appears in top
stacking pairs (N-BEATS+TiDE is the best 2-model combination). Its MLP-based
architecture produces errors that are partially orthogonal to those of
recurrent and convolutional models, making it a useful diversity contributor
even when individually weak.

\textbf{Danger of simple averaging.} At $L=45$, simple averaging
(\textit{worse} than the best individual model by 0.50 pp MAPE) demonstrates
a practical pitfall: combining a strong model (TCN MAPE 3.85\%) with a very
weak one (iTransformer MAPE 8.53\%) via equal weights shifts predictions toward
the low-quality tail. Practitioners should always use at least optimised
weights when model quality is non-uniform.

\textbf{Horizon changes optimal model ranking.} At $L=45$, TCN dominates and
TiDE is near last. At $L=200$, TCN and N-BEATS are nearly tied and TiDE
recovers. This shift---captured by the different optimised weight vectors
(TCN=0.476 at $L=45$ vs.\ 0.126 at $L=200$)---shows that horizon length is a
critical hyperparameter that changes which architecture is best suited to the
task.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We presented a systematic study of six deep learning architectures with four
ensemble strategies for multi-step HR forecasting.  Key conclusions:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
  \item TCN is the best individual model at $L=45$; N-BEATS closely approaches
        it at $L=200$.  iTransformer fails in the univariate setting.
  \item Ridge stacking consistently outperforms optimisation and averaging at
        both horizons and across all 31 model subsets in the permutation search.
  \item Simple averaging is \emph{harmful} when model quality is non-uniform;
        practitioners must use weighted or stacking methods.
  \item A 2-model stacking pair (N-BEATS+TiDE, MAPE=4.627\%) outperforms the
        full 5-model optimisation ensemble (MAPE=4.977\%), showing ensemble
        quality dominates ensemble size.
  \item AIS-Ensemble provides per-subject adaptation and deployment-time OOD
        detection, offering reliability indicators beyond aggregate MAPE.
\end{enumerate}

Future work will incorporate physiological covariates (power, cadence, RPE) as
conditioning signals, evaluate fine-tuned foundation models (MOMENT, Moirai),
explore online AIS memory updates as sessions accumulate, and extend the
framework to probabilistic HR forecasting.
